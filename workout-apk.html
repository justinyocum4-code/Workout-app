Nutrition basics

Simple food guidance to support strength and muscle tone. This is guidance only—there is no food tracking.

Your daily protein starting range

Add your body weight in the fitness questionnaire to calculate a personal range.

A practical range for healthy adults who strength train is about 0.7 to 0.9 grams per pound of body weight each day. Spread it across meals instead of trying to get it all at once.

Build a steady plate

At most meals, combine a protein food, a fruit or vegetable, and a carbohydrate that helps fuel training. Add a little fat for flavor and staying power.

  • Protein: chicken, fish, eggs, Greek yogurt, cottage cheese, beans, lentils, tofu, or lean beef.
  • Training fuel: oats, rice, potatoes, fruit, whole-grain bread, or tortillas.
  • Produce: choose any fruit or vegetable you enjoy and can eat consistently.

Easy meal ideas for toning

  • Greek yogurt, berries, and oats.
  • Eggs, whole-grain toast, and fruit.
  • Chicken or tofu, rice, and mixed vegetables.
  • Tuna or mashed-bean wrap with salad.
  • Salmon or lean beef, potatoes, and a vegetable.
  • Cottage cheese, fruit, and a handful of nuts.

Keep it workable

Drink water regularly, eat enough to recover, and use portions you can repeat. “Toning” comes from building muscle while gradually managing body fat—not from a special food or extreme restriction.

Health note: If you have kidney disease, are pregnant, have a history of disordered eating, or have been given a medical diet, ask a qualified clinician before changing protein intake.

Learn the basics

Short explanations for a first strength program. Each topic is written in plain language and read in a logical order by TalkBack.

Sets and reps

A rep, short for repetition, is one complete movement. One squat down and back up is one rep.

A set is a group of reps done before you rest. “3 sets of 10 reps” means do 10 reps, rest, then repeat that group two more times.

Why rest days matter

Training gives your muscles a reason to adapt. Rest gives them time to repair and become more capable.

A rest day does not require staying still. Easy walking or gentle mobility is fine if it feels good. Avoid hard training for the same sore muscles on back-to-back days.

How progression works

Progression means asking your body to do a little more over time—not making every workout exhausting.

  1. First, learn the movement and finish with about two good reps left.
  2. Then add one or two reps while your form stays steady.
  3. When the top of the rep range feels controlled, use the next available weight or a slightly harder variation.
  4. Change only one thing at a time: reps, weight, range, or an extra set.

What good effort feels like

The last few reps may feel slow and challenging, but you should still control the movement. Stop a set when your form changes.

Muscle effort and normal fatigue are expected. Sharp, pinching, sudden, or worsening pain is a reason to stop and choose a different movement.

Breathing and tempo

For most strength exercises, inhale during the easier or lowering part and exhale during the hardest part. Keep breathing during holds—never strain while holding your breath.

A beginner-friendly tempo is about three seconds to lower, a short pause, then one to two seconds to lift. Slow down whenever speed starts to replace control.

Bodyweight progression paths

Move forward only when every rep is controlled and pain-free. It is always fine to stay on a step longer or move back one step.

  • Push-up: wall push-up → high incline push-up → kneeling push-up → full push-up.
  • Squat: bench sit-to-stand → partial bodyweight squat → full bodyweight squat → supported reverse lunge.
  • Core: bench plank → kneeling shoulder tap → full plank → full plank shoulder tap.
  • Pull-up: supported dead hang → scapular pull-up → band-assisted pull-up → full pull-up.

Train smart before you train hard

  • Warm up for 3 to 5 minutes with easy marching, arm circles, and unloaded versions of your first movement.
  • Choose a resistance that leaves about two good repetitions in reserve. Form should stay steady from first rep to last.
  • Stop if you feel sharp, pinching, or sudden pain. Muscle effort is expected; joint pain is not.
  • For ab roller work, shorten the rollout as soon as your lower back starts to arch.
Move at your own pace. Consistency beats a perfect workout.
